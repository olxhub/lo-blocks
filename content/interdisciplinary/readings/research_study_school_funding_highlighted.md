## The Effects of School `Spending` on Educational and `Economic Outcomes`: Evidence from School `Finance` Reforms 

### Metadata

**Authors**: C. Kirabo Jackson, Rucker C. Johnson, & Claudia Persico  **Journal:** Quarterly `Journal of Economics` **Publication Year**: 2016 

### Abstract
This  study  examines  the  long-term  causal  effects  of  increased  school  `spending`  following  court-ordered  school  `finance`  reforms  enacted  across  US  states  since  the  1970s. By  linking  nationally  representative  longitudinal  data  on  children's  education  with  adult  `socioeconomic`  outcomes,  the  study  tests  whether  sustained  `spending`  increases  improve  high  school  graduation,  educational  attainment,  wages,  and  poverty  status. Results  show  large,  lasting  benefits—particularly  for  children  from  `low-income`  families.  

### Introduction
The  paper  addresses  the  long-standing  question  of  whether "`money  matters`"  in  education  by  exploiting  quasi-experimental  variation  in  school  `funding`  caused  by  state-level  school  `finance`  reforms. It  highlights  how  `resource`  disparities  hinder  equal  opportunity  and  investigates  whether  additional  `funds`  narrow  achievement  and  attainment  gaps.  

### Method
The  authors  use  an  instrumental-variables  strategy  within  a  difference-in-differences  framework. Data  come  from  the  Panel  Study  of  `Income`  Dynamics,  covering  cohorts  born  between  1955  and  1985,  linked  with  school  district  `finance`  records. The  study  estimates  effects  of  sustained  per-pupil  `spending`  increases  over  a  student's  12  years  of  schooling  on  long-run  outcomes.  

### Results
Educational  attainment: A  10%  increase  in  per-pupil  `spending`  sustained  for  all  12  school  years  increased  completed  schooling  by  0. 27  years  and  raised  the  likelihood  of  high  school  graduation.  
Adult  outcomes: Exposure  to  this  increase  raised  adult  `wages`  by  7. 25%,  boosted  family  `income`  by  9. 8%,  and  reduced  annual  `poverty`  incidence  by  3. 67  percentage  points.  
Distributional  effects: `Low-income ` children  saw  the  largest  gains—0. 44  more  years  of  schooling,  9. 6%  higher  wages,  and  a  6. 8  percentage-point  drop  in  `poverty`  rates.  
Mechanisms: `Spending`  increases  translated  into  smaller  class  sizes,  longer  school  years,  and  higher  teacher  `salaries`,  which  contributed  to  improved  student  outcomes.  

### Discussion
The  findings  provide  strong  causal  evidence  that  `money  matters`,  with  educational  and  life-course  benefits  comparable  to  or  greater  than  those  of  targeted  interventions  like  early  childhood  program  or  class  size  reductions. The  results  underscore  the  role  of  equitable  school  `finance`  as  a  central  policy  tool  for  reducing  inequality.  

### Conclusion
Sustained  increases  in  school  `spending`  significantly  improve  high  school  graduation,  long-term  educational  attainment,  adult  `earnings`,  and  `poverty`  outcomes,  especially  for  disadvantaged  students. The  study  demonstrates  that  progressive  school  `finance`  reforms  are  effective  levers  for  both  educational  equity  and  `economic`  mobility.
