## Determinants of Homelessness in the U.S.: New Hypotheses and Evidence

**Authors:** Richard J. Cebula and James W. Saunoris
**Journal:** Applied Economics (2021), 53(49), 5695–5709
**DOI:** 10.1080/00036846.2021.1927970

**Abstract (Summary):**
This study examines determinants of homelessness in the United States by testing two institutional economic hypotheses: that higher levels of entrepreneurial activity and greater labor market freedom are associated with lower homelessness rates. Using state-level panel data and dynamic econometric techniques, the study evaluates whether changes in these economic conditions precede changes in homelessness. Findings provide strong empirical support for both hypotheses. Homelessness rates are found to decrease as entrepreneurial activity and labor market freedom increase, suggesting that institutional economic structures may shape homelessness outcomes.

**Introduction:**
Homelessness in the United States remains a persistent social and economic issue affecting hundreds of thousands of individuals annually. Much of the existing literature focuses on housing affordability, poverty, unemployment, and social welfare provision as key determinants. This study expands the scope of analysis by incorporating broader institutional and market-oriented economic factors that may influence homelessness rates.

**Background:**
Prior research commonly links homelessness to high housing costs, unemployment, income inequality, and reductions in public assistance. However, institutional features of state economies—such as the degree of entrepreneurial activity and the level of labor market regulation—have received limited attention. The study proposes that environments conducive to entrepreneurship may create greater economic opportunity, while more flexible labor markets may reduce barriers to employment, thereby decreasing vulnerability to homelessness.

**Method:**
The study uses state-level panel data across U.S. states to test two hypotheses:

1. Homelessness decreases as entrepreneurial activity increases.
2. Homelessness decreases as labor market freedom increases.

Dynamic econometric methods are employed, including Panel Vector Autoregression (PVAR), Granger causality testing, and Cholesky forecast-error variance decomposition. These techniques allow for examination of dynamic relationships and potential causal direction between institutional economic variables and homelessness rates over time.

**Results:**
Results show strong empirical support for both hypotheses. States with higher levels of entrepreneurial activity exhibit lower homelessness rates. Similarly, greater labor market freedom is associated with lower levels of homelessness. Granger causality tests indicate that changes in entrepreneurial activity and labor market freedom precede changes in homelessness rates, suggesting directional influence rather than simple correlation.

**Discussion:**
The findings indicate that homelessness is associated not only with housing markets and social policy, but also with broader institutional economic conditions. By introducing measures of entrepreneurial activity and labor market freedom, the study broadens structural explanations of homelessness to include economic opportunity structures and regulatory environments.

**Conclusion:**
Homelessness rates are negatively associated with entrepreneurial activity and labor market freedom at the state level. Institutional economic design appears to play a role in shaping homelessness outcomes. Policies that promote entrepreneurship and labor market flexibility may complement housing and welfare interventions in efforts to reduce homelessness.
